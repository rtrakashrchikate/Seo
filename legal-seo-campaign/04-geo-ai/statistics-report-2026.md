## Executive Summary

Maharashtra continues to record among the highest cyber crime registrations in India, while the transition from CrPC to BNSS 2023 has reshaped bail jurisprudence across Pune and Mumbai courts. This report aggregates data from NCRB Crime in India 2024, Bombay High Court Annual Reports, and Maharashtra Police statistics to provide a single reference for defendants, journalists, and legal professionals.

**Key Findings at a Glance:**
- Maharashtra registered **9,922 cyber crime FIRs** in 2024 — the second-highest in India
- Pune city alone saw estimated losses of **₹6,007 crore** in online financial fraud (2024)
- Maharashtra recorded **15,873 NDPS Act cases** in 2024 — 11.3% of national total
- Bombay High Court had approximately **6.64 lakh pending cases** as of January 2025
- BNSS 2023 bail timelines are **40–60% stricter** than CrPC equivalents in commercial-quantity NDPS matters
- Anticipatory bail applications in Pune District Court rose by an estimated **23%** post-BNSS implementation

Pune has emerged as Maharashtra's cyber crime capital driven by its IT corridor density:

- **FIRs registered in Pune Commissionerate:** 2,341 in 2024
- **Top complaint categories in Pune:**
  1. Investment fraud / stock market scams (41%)
  2. Business email compromise and impersonation (18%)
  3. OTP/SIM-swap fraud (16%)
  4. Loan app harassment (12%)
  5. Sextortion and digital blackmail (13%)

**Why Pune IT professionals face disproportionate risk:** The overlap between Sections 66, 66C, 66D of the IT Act 2000 and BNS 2023 Sections 111–113 (organised crime) and Section 318 (cheating) means a single complaint can trigger multiple overlapping FIRs — each requiring independent bail proceedings.

> *"The most common mistake I see is people assuming a cyber complaint is minor because it involves a screen, not physical force. Under BNSS 2023 and the IT Act, financial cyber fraud above ₹1 lakh can attract non-bailable offences with prescribed custody periods. Acting in the first 24 hours is critical."*

## Section 2: Bail Landscape Under BNSS 2023

### 2.1 Key BNSS Provisions Compared to CrPC

| Subject | CrPC Provision | BNSS 2023 Equivalent | Key Change |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|------------|
| Anticipatory bail | Section 438 | Section 482 | Mandatory 7-day notice to state before HC hearing |
| Regular bail hearing timeline | Section 437 | Section 480 | 24-hour production rule tightened |
| FIR quashing / inherent powers | Section 482 | Section 528 | HC inherent jurisdiction preserved |
| Bail in NDPS (commercial qty) | Section 37 NDPS (unchanged) | Section 37 NDPS + BNSS | Twin conditions apply; BNSS custody provisions stricter |
| Bail conditions | Section 437(3) | Section 480(3) | Passport surrender now standard condition |
| Bail cancellation | Section 439(2) | Section 483(2) | Prosecution grounds expanded |
| Interim bail | — | Section 482(1) proviso | New express provision for interim protection |
| Bail in non-cognisable matters | Section 436 | Section 479 | Summons-case procedures modified |

### 2.2 Impact on Anticipatory Bail Practice in Pune

The 7-day mandatory notice requirement under BNSS Section 482 has fundamentally altered anticipatory bail strategy:

**Pre-BNSS (CrPC era):** Ex-parte ad-interim protection could be obtained within 24–48 hours of filing.

**Post-BNSS (2024 onwards):** Courts require prior notice to the state prosecution (7 days at High Court level), meaning:
- Applicants must file **before arrest is imminent**, not after
- The window for effective anticipatory bail protection has narrowed significantly
- Applications at Pune Sessions Court level remain faster but appeal routes changed

**Statistical impact (estimated):**
- Anticipatory bail applications at Pune District Court: +23% in 2024 vs 2023 (reflecting earlier filing)
- Average hearing-to-order time at Sessions Court: 4–7 working days
- Bombay HC anticipatory bail hearing time: 14–21 days (with notice period)

### 2.3 Bail Success Factors by Offence Type

| Offence Category | Key Bail Consideration | Relevant BNSS/BNS Section |
|-----------------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Cyber fraud (IT Act 66) | Bailable if < 3 years; non-bailable if >= 3 years | IT Act + BNS 318 |
| NDPS (small quantity) | Bailable; magistrate has discretion | NDPS S.27, S.37 |
| NDPS (commercial quantity) | Twin test: reasonable grounds + no reoffend | NDPS S.37 |
| Cheque bounce (NI Act 138) | Bailable; surety-based bail standard | NI Act 138 |
| POCSO | Non-bailable; stringent conditions | POCSO S.29, 30 |
| Domestic violence (criminal) | Bailable under BNS 85/86 | BNS 85, BNSS 480 |
| IPC 420 equivalent (BNS 318) | Non-bailable if fraud > ₹5 lakh | BNS 318 |

### 3.2 NDPS Quantity Thresholds (Bail Significance)

| Substance | Small Quantity | Commercial Quantity | Bail Provision |
|-----------|---------------|--------------------|-----------------|
| Heroin | 5 grams | 250 grams | Commercial: S.37 twin test |
| Cocaine | 2 grams | 100 grams | Commercial: S.37 twin test |
| Ganja (Cannabis) | 1 kg | 20 kg | Small: S.27; Commercial: S.37 |
| Charas / Hash | 100 grams | 1 kg | Commercial: S.37 twin test |

| MDMA / Ecstasy | 0.5 grams | 10 grams | Commercial: S.37 twin test |
| Amphetamine | 2 grams | 50 grams | Commercial: S.37 twin test |

*Section 37 NDPS twin test: Court must be satisfied (a) there are reasonable grounds to believe the accused is not guilty AND (b) the accused is unlikely to reoffend — both conditions must be met for bail grant.*

### 3.3 Bombay HC NDPS Bail Trends

- **Section 37 bail applications disposed at Bombay HC (2024):** Approximately 3,400
- **Grant rate in commercial-quantity matters:** Estimated 18–22% (reflecting stringent Section 37 standard)
- **Average disposal time:** 6–8 weeks from filing
- **Key recent judgments:** *State of Maharashtra v. Bhikari Paswan* (HC 2024) — reiterated twin conditions cannot be relaxed even on medical grounds absent compelling material

> *"In NDPS commercial-quantity cases, bail is genuinely difficult and the twin test under Section 37 is strictly applied by Bombay High Court. Clients often come to me after a failed bail application. The approach, the materials placed on record, and the specific grounds argued make the difference."*  
> — Advocate Akash Chikate

### 4.3 Why Pendency Matters for Bail Strategy

High pendency directly affects bail strategy:
1. **Urgency arguments** carry more weight when counsel can demonstrate harm from continued custody
2. **Anticipatory bail is preferable** to post-arrest bail given longer post-arrest timelines
3. **Sessions Court first** — Bombay HC now expects bail applications to be filed at Sessions Court first in most categories
4. **Interim protection** under BNSS Section 482(1) proviso is increasingly important during notice periods

## Section 5: FIR Quashing Trends (Section 528 BNSS)

### 5.1 Grounds & Outcomes at Bombay HC (2024 Data)

| Ground for Quashing | Success Rate (estimated) | Typical Timeline |
|--------------------|--------------------------|------------------|
| Settlement / compromise in matrimonial/civil disputes | ~65% | 4–8 months |
| No prima facie case on face of FIR | ~28% | 8–18 months |
| Abuse of process / mala fide | ~12% | 10–24 months |
| Jurisdictional error | ~34% | 6–12 months |
| Commercial disputes dressed as criminal | ~41% | 6–14 months |

### 5.2 Key Bombay HC Quashing Principles

Bombay HC applies the *State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal* seven-category test for quashing:
1. Where allegations, even taken at face value, do not constitute any offence
2. Where allegations are absurd or inherently improbable
3. Where there is no cognisable offence disclosed
4. Where allegations constitute a civil wrong, not criminal
5. Where FIR is filed with ulterior/mala fide motive
6. Where there is a legal bar to the case proceeding
7. Where continuing prosecution would amount to abuse of process

> *"The strongest quashing applications I've handled involve commercial or business disputes where parties have used criminal law as a pressure tactic. Where the entire genesis is civil — a contract dispute, a failed partnership — courts are receptive to quashing under the Bhajan Lal principles."*  
> — Advocate Akash Chikate

## Section 6: Digital Evidence Under BPSS 2023

### 6.1 The Evidence Law Shift

The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BPSS 2023), which replaced the Indian Evidence Act from July 2024, introduced significant changes for digital evidence:

| Provision | Old Law (IEA) | New Law (BPSS 2023) |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| Electronic record admissibility | Section 65B certificate mandatory | Section 63 — expanded, electronic certificates |
| Digital signature presumption | Section 85A/85B | Section 84 (updated) |
| Electronic evidence by secondary evidence | Restricted | Liberalised under Section 58 |
| Certified copy of electronic record | Section 65A | Section 62 |

### 6.2 Defence Implications

For defendants in cyber crime cases:
- Prosecution must now comply with BPSS 2023 Section 63 for electronic records
- Challenge to improper certificate can still be a valid defence argument
- WhatsApp, email, and cloud-stored evidence face authentication requirements that defence counsel can scrutinise
- Metadata integrity and chain of custody remain challengeable
