## Key Provisions — PC Act 1988 (as amended 2018)

| Section | Offence | Punishment |
|---------|---------|------------|
| PC Act 7 | Public servant accepting gratification (bribe) | 3 to 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 7A | Taking undue advantage to influence public servant | 3 to 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 8 | Bribe-giver (giving gratification) | 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 9 | Bribe through a commercial organisation | 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 11 | Public servant obtaining valuables without consideration | 3 to 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 13(1)(a) | Habitual acceptance of gratification | 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 13(1)(b) | Disproportionate assets | 7 years + fine |
| PC Act 17A | Investigation sanction requirement | Prosecution sanction needed |

**All PC Act offences are cognisable and non-bailable.** Trial occurs before Special Courts designated under the PC Act.

## The 2018 Amendment — Critical Changes

The 2018 amendment to the PC Act introduced significant changes:

1. **Bribe-giving is now a criminal offence (Section 8):** Prior to 2018, only the public servant accepting a bribe was criminally liable. Now the person who pays a bribe is also liable (3–7 years). Exception: if the person was compelled to pay and reports it to law enforcement within 7 days.

2. **Section 17A — Prosecution Sanction:** Before any investigation of a public servant (present or former) for acts done in official capacity, the investigating agency must obtain sanction from the competent government authority. Failure to obtain prior sanction is a ground to challenge the entire investigation.

3. **Disproportionate Assets (Section 13(1)(b)):** A public servant possessing assets disproportionate to their known sources of income (including assets of family members) is guilty of criminal misconduct.

## ACB Trap Cases — How They Work

The most common PC Act prosecution in Maharashtra is an ACB (Anti-Corruption Bureau) trap case:

1. **Complaint:** A complainant approaches ACB alleging a public servant demanded a bribe.
2. **Trap preparation:** ACB prepares trap currency (phenolphthalein powder applied to notes).
3. **Trap laid:** The complainant pays the bribe with the prepared notes in ACB's presence.
4. **Arrest:** ACB apprehends the public servant; hands test positive for phenolphthalein.
5. **Panchanama:** ACB records a detailed panchanama of the trap proceedings.
6. **FIR and prosecution:** ACB registers FIR and files chargesheet before Special Court.

## Defence in ACB Trap Cases

ACB trap cases have specific defence angles that experienced counsel examines:

**1. Demand Not Established**
Section 7 PC Act requires proof that the accused **demanded** the gratification — not merely that they received it. Where the accused denies demand and the only evidence is the complainant's word, the absence of independent corroboration of demand is a significant defence point.

**2. No Voluntary Acceptance**
If the currency was thrust upon the accused or placed near them without their knowledge or acceptance, the prosecution case is weakened. Courts have held that mere possession of trap currency without voluntary acceptance does not establish the offence.

**3. Phenolphthalein Test — Contamination**
The phenolphthalein test is not infallible. It can produce false positives from contact with other substances. Defence examines the test procedure, chain of custody of the trap currency, and whether the panchanama records are consistent.

**4. Complainant's Motive**
ACB complaints are sometimes motivated by personal enmity, vendetta, or to escape legitimate government action against the complainant. Where there is a demonstrable motive for a false complaint, this is material to the defence.

**5. Independent Witness Issues**
ACB traps require independent witnesses (panchas). Where the panchas are police department employees or have prior ACB involvement, their independence can be challenged.

**6. Section 17A — Sanction Absence**
For acts done by public servants in their official capacity, prior sanction under Section 17A PC Act is mandatory before investigation. Absence of proper sanction is a ground to challenge the prosecution.

## Disproportionate Assets (DA) Cases

DA cases under Section 13(1)(b) PC Act are complex prosecutions where the charge is that the public servant's total assets (including those in family members' names) exceed their known income during their service period.

**Key defence arguments in DA cases:**
- Income from legitimate sources not accounted for by prosecution (agricultural income, inherited property, gifts)
- Gifts and inheritances properly documented
- Assets belong to family members who have independent income sources
- Valuation of property challenged (prosecution uses higher valuations; defence uses contemporaneous market rates)
- Known sources of income calculated correctly
- Calculation period challenged

## Bail in PC Act Cases

PC Act offences are non-bailable. Bail applications are heard by the Special Court (PC Act) or Sessions Court, and the Bombay High Court on appeal.

**Factors favouring bail in PC Act matters:**
- Accused is a first-time offender with no criminal history
- The trap case evidence has specific procedural defects
- Accused has cooperated with investigation
- Long custody period without trial completion
- The accused is a low-ranking public servant with limited assets alleged
- Prosecution sanction under Section 17A obtained improperly

## CBI Matters — Jurisdiction and Process

CBI investigates PC Act matters where:
- Central government servants are accused
- The matter is transferred from ACB by High Court or Supreme Court
- Multi-state or inter-state corruption is involved

CBI investigations follow a similar pattern to ACB but with federal resources. Defence in CBI matters requires understanding of the separate CBI Manual procedures and the Special CBI Courts in Mumbai (which also handle Pune-originating CBI cases).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is paying a bribe a criminal offence?**
Yes — since the 2018 PC Act amendment, Section 8 makes bribe-giving a criminal offence punishable with up to 7 years imprisonment. However, there is an exception: if a person was compelled to give a bribe and voluntarily reports it to law enforcement within 7 days, they are not liable for prosecution.

**Q: What is the Section 17A sanction and why does it matter?**
Section 17A PC Act requires prior sanction from the competent government authority before investigating a public servant for acts done in their official capacity. The Supreme Court has held this is a mandatory pre-condition. If the investigating agency proceeded without sanction, the entire investigation and prosecution may be liable to be quashed.

**Q: Can a PC Act case be quashed at Bombay High Court?**
Yes — PC Act cases can be quashed under Section 528 BNSS on grounds including: absence of sanction under Section 17A, no demand established on the face of the FIR, mala fide complaint, or where the entire prosecution case is inherently improbable. Each case depends on its specific facts.

**Q: What is an ACB trap case?**
An ACB (Anti-Corruption Bureau) trap case is a sting operation where the ACB, acting on a complaint from a citizen, prepares marked currency and facilitates payment of a demanded bribe to catch a public servant red-handed. The trap is documented in a panchanama by ACB officers and independent witnesses.

**Q: I am a government servant who received currency without demanding it. What should I do?**
Contact a criminal defence lawyer immediately. Do not make any statement to ACB or police without counsel. Whether you demanded the currency is a central question in the prosecution — your defence must be carefully structured from the outset. Early legal intervention is critical in trap cases.

**Q: How long do PC Act trials take in Pune?**
PC Act trials before the Special Court in Pune typically take 3–8 years from chargesheet to judgment, depending on the number of witnesses and complexity of documents. Bail during the trial period is therefore particularly important.
